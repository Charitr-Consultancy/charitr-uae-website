Designing Learning Around the People Who Use It

Most organisations in Dubai have no shortage of training content. Onboarding modules, compliance courses and leadership programmes can all be built and rolled out quickly. The harder question for business owners and executives is whether any of it changes how people work. Effective digital learning begins with real needs and clear, accessible experiences, and that starts with the people using it.

Start with the learner’s context

Before commissioning a programme or choosing a platform, understand who it is for. Three questions shape almost every decision that follows:

  • What are their goals? Employees rarely want to “complete a course”. They want to serve clients better, take on a bigger role or meet a standard. Learning tied to those goals earns attention, and it supports your business objectives at the same time.
  • How much time do they have? A senior manager between client meetings and a new graduate in an induction week need different things. Design for the time people actually have, not the time the training calendar assumes.
  • What do they already know? Dubai teams often bring varied experience from many countries and industries. A short pre-assessment or a few conversations with team leads can show where to begin, so experienced people aren’t held back and newer colleagues aren’t left behind.

Context also covers language, devices and connectivity. A multilingual, mobile-first workforce needs learning that works on a phone, uses plain language and respects different levels of familiarity with technology.

Organise around clear outcomes

Define what people should be able to do after the learning, in terms the business cares about: “prepare a client risk summary”, “lead a performance conversation”, “handle a service escalation”. Outcomes written as actions give learners a clear target and give you a way to judge whether the investment is working.

Every section should then support an outcome. If a topic doesn’t help someone do the job better, make it optional reading rather than a required lesson.

Make room to practise

People rarely build skills by watching alone. Build in realistic opportunities to apply what they learn, such as short scenarios drawn from your own clients and markets, reflection prompts, or small tasks connected to current projects. Practice does not need to be elaborate. A few well-chosen activities that mirror real situations will do more than a long list of generic questions.

Make navigation accessible

Accessibility is part of good design, not an add-on. Clear headings, readable text, strong colour contrast, captions on video and keyboard-friendly navigation help everyone, including colleagues with disabilities and anyone learning on a small screen or in a noisy environment.

Simple navigation matters equally. Learners should always know where they are, what comes next and how to return to a topic. For busy professionals, every minute spent working out the interface is a minute taken from learning.

Let learner feedback guide improvement

No programme is right the first time. Learners can tell you where an explanation was unclear, where a task felt irrelevant or where the pace was wrong. Short surveys, informal conversations with managers and patterns in the data, such as where people drop out, all provide useful signals.

Treat that feedback as a guide for targeted refinements rather than a reason to start over. Small, regular improvements steadily make a programme more effective and show your teams that their input matters.

Keep the learner at the centre

For leaders, designing learning around the people who use it is a practical business decision. When learning fits real needs, schedules and abilities, people complete it, apply it and benefit from it, and the organisation sees the return. Start with your people’s context, define the outcomes that matter, and let their experience shape what you build next.
