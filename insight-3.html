From Business Need to Purposeful Software

Many software projects begin with a solution: a platform someone saw demonstrated, an app a competitor launched, a tool the IT team recommends. Months later, the business has a system that works as designed but doesn’t quite fit how people work. For business owners and executives, the way to avoid this is simple to state and harder to practise. A clear problem definition provides a stronger starting point for digital development.

Clarify the task before choosing the technology

Technology should be the last decision, not the first. Before any vendor conversations or feature lists, be able to say in a sentence or two what the software must help people do. For example:

  • “Our advisers spend hours compiling client reports by hand, and the format varies by person.”
  • “Approvals for new engagements take too long because requests move between inboxes.”

A statement like this keeps the project honest. It shows what success looks like, helps you judge whether a proposed solution is relevant, and gives the team something to return to when new ideas appear.

Map the current workflow

Software supports a process, so understand the process as it really runs, not as the policy document describes it. Trace the work step by step: who starts it, who touches it, where it waits, and where information is re-entered or lost.

This exercise often surfaces surprises. Some delays turn out to be caused by unclear responsibilities rather than missing technology. Some steps exist only because of an old habit. Fixing these first can make the eventual software simpler, cheaper and more effective. Automating a confusing process only makes the confusion faster.

Involve the people who use it

The people doing the work hold knowledge that no requirements document captures: the workarounds they rely on, the exceptions that happen every week, the points where errors creep in. Involve them early, through short interviews, observation or a working session, and keep them involved as the solution takes shape.

This improves the design, and it also builds trust. People are far more likely to adopt a tool they helped shape than one that arrives fully formed. In organisations with teams across different functions and locations, bringing those voices together can also reveal that departments define the same problem differently.

Prioritise essential requirements

Every stakeholder will have a wish list, and most of it will be reasonable. The discipline lies in separating what is essential from what is merely desirable. Ask of each requirement: if this were missing at launch, could the task still be done? If the answer is yes, it can wait.

A short, ranked list of essentials keeps the project focused, controls cost and makes decisions easier when trade-offs arise. Items that are deferred aren’t discarded. They simply wait until the core is proven.

Build in small, testable increments

Large launches concentrate risk. A smaller approach lowers it: release a limited version to a small group, observe how it performs against the original problem, and learn before expanding. Each increment should be small enough to test in real conditions and meaningful enough to deliver value.

This approach gives leaders clearer evidence at each stage. You can see what is working, adjust the direction early and avoid committing the full budget before the idea has been tested. It also keeps the software tied to the original need rather than drifting towards features no one asked for.

Purpose first, then platform

Purposeful software is not necessarily the most sophisticated or the most expensive. It is software that solves a defined problem for the people who use it, and can be shown to do so. Start by describing the need clearly, understand the work as it happens, involve your teams, focus on essentials and grow in tested steps. When those foundations are in place, the choice of technology becomes far easier, and far more likely to pay off.
